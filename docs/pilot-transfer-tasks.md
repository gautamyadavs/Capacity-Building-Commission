# Provisional matched transfer tasks

These two fictional tasks accompany [the pilot protocol](learning-pilot.md). They are research materials outside the application, not new learner cases or a validated assessment. A subject-matter reviewer should check their clarity and plausibility before recruitment. Counterbalance A-before/B-after and B-before/A-after. Neither task has a single required policy action. Human reviewers use the explicit reasoning criteria in the protocol and should not grade writing mechanics.

## Administration

Allow approximately 8–10 active minutes per form, using the same allowance before and after. Record actual duration and any accommodation or facilitator assistance. Provide Part 1 alone. Keep its answer as the original submission before revealing Part 2; the participant may read it but cannot edit it. Use separate answer sheets or study files. Do not show commentary, sample answers or ratings until both pre/post tasks are complete. The fictional rules below are the only policy requirements for this task; external sector knowledge is unnecessary.

The same response prompts and maxima apply to both forms. A maximum is not a target. Participants may use bullet points and may pause; record breaks separately. Supply both sets of facts during Part 2. Do not supply facts from the learning pilot or suggest which lesson to use.

## Form A: delayed household support payments

### Part 1: situation and initial decision

You lead a district team responding to a backlog of household support payments. Decide how to organise the next three working days.

- There are 1,200 pending applications. About 300 applicants report that they cannot meet essential household expenses if payment is delayed for another two working days. These reports have not all been checked.
- Six review teams are available. Each usually completes about 80 applications per day when records are complete. There is no reliable estimate for applications needing extra checks.
- In this fictional programme, payment can be released only after identity and eligibility are verified. A payment that is released incorrectly is difficult to recover. Applicants must be told what is missing when an application is held.
- An automated flag marks about one fifth of pending applications as possible duplicate claims. The estimate comes from a small convenience sample; the false-flag rate is uncertain. A flag is not a determination that a claim is invalid.
- A public help desk can receive documents but cannot authorise payment. Review teams verify claims; the finance office releases payments. Applicants have received inconsistent explanations of the delay.
- An applicant group asks that urgent claims be dealt with first. The finance office warns that prioritising those claims may leave older routine applications waiting longer.

**Response, maximum 120 words:** What problem would you prioritise over the next three working days, and how would you organise a defensible response? Explain your reasons, one accepted trade-off, and one observable result you expect or critical assumption you would monitor.

### Part 2: update and challenge

Two working days later:

- A team assigned to urgent claims has completed 150 applications. Many were selected because their records were already complete; this does not describe the full urgent group.
- In 60 flagged applications that were reviewed, 42 flags were caused by repeated uploads from the same applicant. The reviewed group was not a random sample of all flagged applications.
- The help desk reports fewer queries at its counter. Phone queues remain long, and there is no complete count of applicants who have given up trying to contact it.
- Several applicants still awaiting review report increasing hardship. Teams say that following up incomplete records takes more time than expected.

A senior official argues: “The reviewed flags were mostly harmless and counter queues have fallen. Stop spending time on the flags and put all teams on urgent claims. That is the fastest way to reduce hardship.”

**Response, maximum 100 words:** For the next three working days, what would you change and what would you retain? Explain what the update supports and leaves uncertain. Respond to the official's strongest argument, name the important trade-off you accept, and state one observable condition that would make you reconsider.

## Matching and interpretation

Both forms have the same response structure, horizons, resource capacity, competing queue demands, uncertain flag estimate, divided responsibilities, selected update sample and counterargument. Context and consequences differ. Matching is provisional: review domain familiarity, perceived stakes, response time and human ratings by form before treating them as equivalent.

Look for reasons that connect facts to actions, acknowledge resource and institutional limits, distinguish selected observations from a wider conclusion, engage the strongest challenge and state a meaningful monitoring condition. Do not award points for a predetermined allocation or for reproducing the pilot commentary. A participant can defend different allocations while respecting the fictional verification requirement.

The second form may benefit from familiarity with the first form's structure. Counterbalancing and human review reduce some ambiguity, but this small pre/post exercise cannot separate practice effects from the learning pilot's effect. Report it as an investigation of reasoning on an unfamiliar task, alongside the protocol's limitations.
